Fisher's Sampling Theorem

ST229 · Section 7.4 · the proof, as a rotation of the sample
  • (a)step 2
    \(\bar X\sim N\!\left(\mu,\ \sigma^2/n\right)\)
  • (b)step 5
    \(\dfrac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-1}\)
  • (c)step 5
    \(\bar X\ \text{and}\ S^2\ \text{are independent}\)
  • (d)step 6
    \(\dfrac{\bar X-\mu}{\sqrt{S^2/n}}\sim t_{n-1}\)

One sample is one point in \(\mathbb R^n\)

Setting. \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) are independent \(N(\mu,\sigma^2)\), with \[\bar X=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i,\]\[S^2=\frac1{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-\bar X)^2 .\]

Stack the sample into a single random vector \(\mathbf X=(X_1,\dots,X_n)^T\). Independence makes the covariance matrix diagonal, so

\[\mathbf X\sim \mathrm{MVN}\!\left(\mu\mathbf 1,\ \sigma^2 I_n\right),\qquad \mathbf 1=(1,\dots,1)^T .\]

Each grey dot in the picture is a whole sample of size \(n\). The cloud is round, and its centre \(\mu\mathbf 1\) sits on the diagonal. We follow the violet sample \(\mathbf X\) through the proof.

The whole proof is one idea: choose new axes in which \(\bar X\) is the first coordinate and \(S^2\) is built from all the others.

Try Drag the violet point, or press New sample. Move \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\): \(\mu\) slides the cloud along the diagonal and \(\sigma\) sets its radius.

Split the sample into its mean and what is left

\[\mathbf X=\class{mean}{\bar X\,\mathbf 1}+\class{res}{(\mathbf X-\bar X\,\mathbf 1)}\]

\(\class{mean}{\bar X\mathbf 1}\) is the projection of \(\mathbf X\) onto the diagonal \(\{c\mathbf 1\}\), a 1-dimensional space. Its length is \(\sqrt n\,|\bar X|\).

The residual \(\class{res}{\mathbf X-\bar X\mathbf 1}\) has entries that sum to zero, \(\sum_i (X_i-\bar X)=0\), so it lies in \(\{x:\sum_i x_i=0\}\), which has dimension \(n-1\): a line when \(n=2\), a plane when \(n=3\). Its squared length is

\[\class{res}{\lVert \mathbf X-\bar X\mathbf 1\rVert^2=\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-\bar X)^2=(n-1)S^2 .}\]

Try Use the buttons under the pictures to move \(\mathbf X\) along the diagonal: \(\bar X\) changes and \(S^2\) does not. Move it across: \(S^2\) changes and \(\bar X\) does not. The two statistics are read off from perpendicular directions.

(a) \(\bar X\) is a linear combination of independent Gaussians, so it is Gaussian, with \(\mathbb E[\bar X]=\mu\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\frac1{n^2}\sum_i\sigma^2=\sigma^2/n\).

Rotate so the diagonal becomes the first axis

Take an orthogonal matrix \(O\) whose first row is \(\tfrac1{\sqrt n}\mathbf 1^T\), and set \(\mathbf Y=O\mathbf X\).

\[O=\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac1{\sqrt n}\,\mathbf 1^T\\[4pt] \text{any orthonormal completion}\end{pmatrix}\]\[\text{e.g. } n=2:\quad O=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}\]

Multiplying by \(O\) turns the whole picture rigidly. It is the same sample, described in new axes: \(Y_1\) along the old diagonal, and \(Y_2,\dots,Y_n\) spanning the green line or plane. The first coordinate picks out the mean:

\[\class{mean}{Y_1=\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{j=1}^n X_j=\sqrt n\,\bar X .}\]

Every other row of \(O\) is orthogonal to \(\mathbf 1\), so \(\sum_j O_{ij}=0\) and \(\mathbb E[Y_i]=\mu\sum_j O_{ij}=0\) for \(i\ge 2\).

Try Scrub the rotation slider back and forth. The cloud, the sample and the faded \(X\) axes turn together as one rigid picture, so no length, angle or shape changes. The only thing that changes is which numbers we read off: \((X_1,\dots,X_n)\) before, \((Y_1,\dots,Y_n)\) after.

The cloud is still round, so the new coordinates are independent

\[\Sigma_{\mathbf Y}=O\,\Sigma_{\mathbf X}\,O^T=\sigma^2\,OO^T=\sigma^2 I_n .\]

\(\mathbf Y\) is a linear map of a Gaussian vector, so it is Gaussian. Gaussian with zero covariances means independent. Therefore \(Y_1,\dots,Y_n\) are independent Gaussians with

\[\begin{gathered}\class{mean}{Y_1\sim N(\sqrt n\,\mu,\ \sigma^2)},\\ \class{res}{Y_2,\dots,Y_n\sim N(0,\sigma^2)},\end{gathered}\]

and all \(n\) of them are independent.

This is where normality does the work: a round Gaussian cloud looks the same from every direction, so turning the axes does not create dependence.

Try Press Centre the data, then Rotate back and Rotate. With \(\mu=0\) the cloud sits at the origin and the rotation leaves it visibly unchanged. Nothing is lost by centring: subtracting \(\mu\) from every \(X_i\) leaves \(S^2\) alone and turns \(\bar X\) into \(\bar X-\mu\).

Rotation keeps lengths, which gives (b) and (c)

\[\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i^2=\mathbf Y^T\mathbf Y=\mathbf X^TO^TO\,\mathbf X=\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^2 .\]

Pythagoras in the triangle with corners \(\mathbf 0\), \(\bar X\mathbf 1\), \(\mathbf X\):

\[\begin{aligned}\class{res}{(n-1)S^2}&=\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^2-n\bar X^2\\ &=\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i^2-\class{mean}{Y_1^2}=\class{res}{\sum_{i=2}^n Y_i^2 .}\end{aligned}\]

The mean has used up one of the \(n\) coordinates, which is why \(n-1\) appears.

(c) \(\bar X\) is a function of \(Y_1\) alone and \(S^2\) is a function of \(Y_2,\dots,Y_n\) alone, so they are independent.

(b) \(\dfrac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}=\sum_{i=2}^n\Big(\dfrac{Y_i}{\sigma}\Big)^2\) is a sum of \(n-1\) squared independent \(N(0,1)\) variables, which is \(\chi^2_{n-1}\) by definition.

Try Press New sample a few times. Under each picture the two green numbers always agree.

Assemble the \(t\) statistic, then check by simulation

Divide top and bottom by \(\sqrt{\sigma^2/n}\):

\[\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sqrt{S^2/n}}=\frac{\class{mean}{(\bar X-\mu)\big/\sqrt{\sigma^2/n}}}{\sqrt{\class{res}{\dfrac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}}\Big/(n-1)}}\]

(d) The top is \(N(0,1)\) by (a), the bottom is \(\sqrt{\chi^2_{n-1}/(n-1)}\) by (b), and they are independent by (c). That is the definition of \(t_{n-1}\).

Each run draws 5,000 samples of size \(n\) and compares them with the four claims.

Try Set \(n=2\), then raise it: the \(t_{n-1}\) curve closes in on the dashed \(N(0,1)\). Then switch the data to the skewed distribution: \(\bar X\) and \(S^2\) become dependent and the \(t\) curve stops fitting, because that cloud is not round.
