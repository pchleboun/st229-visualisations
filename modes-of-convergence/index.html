Modes of convergence

ST229 · Chapter 8 · Lectures 17 and 18 · one sequence of random variables, seen four ways
  • Def 8.2Almost surely strongest
    \(\mathbb P\big(\{\omega: X_n(\omega)\to X(\omega)\}\big)=1\)
  • Def 8.1In probability
    \(\forall\varepsilon>0:\ \ \mathbb P\big(|X_n-X|>\varepsilon\big)\to 0\)
  • Def 8.3In distribution weakest
    \(F_{X_n}(x)\to F_X(x)\ \text{ at all continuity points } x \text{ of } F_X\)

Recall: a real sequence converges, \(x_n\to x\), if for any tolerance \(\varepsilon>0\) it is eventually within \(\varepsilon\) of \(x\). Each mode applies that idea to a different real sequence built from \((X_n)\). Almost surely \(\Rightarrow\) in probability \(\Rightarrow\) in distribution.

Choose a sequence

\[\bar X_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i,\qquad X=\mu,\]

where \(X_1,X_2,\dots\) are independent fair coin flips (0 or 1), so \(\mu=\tfrac12\) and \(\sigma^2=\tfrac14\). An outcome \(\omega\) is a whole sequence of flips; the first view shows 300 of them.

Try Press Play. The solid amber curve is the exact probability; the dashed curve is the Chebyshev bound \(\sigma^2/(n\varepsilon^2)\) from the proof of the weak law. Then watch single outcomes in the violet view. Which law is that view about?

In probability: yes (Theorem 8.1, WLLN). \(\mathbb E[\bar X_n]=\mu\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)=\sigma^2/n\), so by Chebyshev \(\mathbb P(|\bar X_n-\mu|\ge\varepsilon)\le\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2}\to0\).

Almost surely: yes (Theorem 8.2, SLLN). With probability 1 the violet path is a convergent real sequence. This is a stronger statement and needs a different proof.

In distribution: yes. \(F_{\bar X_n}\) tends to the step at \(\mu\), the distribution function of the constant \(\mu\).

\[X_n\sim\text{Unif}\{1,2,\dots,n\}\]

built here as \(X_n(\omega)=\lceil n\omega\rceil\); the dependence between different \(n\) does not matter for the distribution.

Try Raise \(n\) and drag \(x\) in the green view. What does \(F_{X_n}(x)\) tend to, for each fixed \(x\)? Is the limit a distribution function?

In distribution: no. \(\mathbb P(X_n=x)=\tfrac1n\to0\) for each \(x\), and \(F_{X_n}(x)=\tfrac{\lfloor x\rfloor}{n}\to0\) for every \(x\). The limit is not a distribution function: probability mass is escaping to infinity.

Almost surely and in probability: no. \(X_n(\omega)\to\infty\) for every \(\omega>0\).

\[Y_n=\frac{X_n}{n}\in[0,1],\qquad X_n\sim\text{Unif}\{1,\dots,n\};\qquad \text{limit: } \text{Unif}[0,1].\]

Built here as \(Y_n(\omega)=\lceil n\omega\rceil/n\). The grey spikes in the green view are the mass function, height \(\tfrac1n\).

In distribution: yes. \(F_{Y_n}(y)=\tfrac{\lfloor ny\rfloor}{n}\), and \(y-\tfrac1n\le\tfrac{\lfloor ny\rfloor}{n}\le y\), so \(F_{Y_n}(y)\to y\) on \([0,1]\). Discrete random variables with a continuous limit.

Almost surely and in probability: yes, for this construction. \(|Y_n(\omega)-\omega|<\tfrac1n\) for every \(\omega\). The example in the notes only asks about the distribution.

with \(\omega\) uniform on \([0,1]\).

Try Drag \(\omega\) towards 1. The closer it is, the longer the violet sequence takes to drop into the band. Does it always get there?

Almost surely: yes. For every \(\omega<1\), \(\omega^n\to0\). The only exception is \(\omega=1\), a set of probability 0, which is why we do not ask for every \(\omega\).

In probability: yes. \(\mathbb P(\omega^n>\varepsilon)=1-\varepsilon^{1/n}\to0\).

In distribution: yes. \(F_{X_n}(x)=x^{1/n}\to1\) for every \(x\in(0,1]\).

\[X_n=\mathbf 1_{A_n},\qquad X=0,\]

where \(A_n\) is an interval of length \(\tfrac1{n+1}\) that starts where \(A_{n-1}\) ended and wraps around \([0,1]\).

Almost surely: no. The lengths \(\tfrac1{n+1}\) sum to infinity, so the block laps \([0,1]\) for ever and covers every \(\omega\) infinitely often.

In probability: yes. \(\mathbb P(|X_n|>\varepsilon)=\tfrac1{n+1}\to0\) for \(\varepsilon<1\). Large errors become rare, but never stop.

In distribution: yes. Implied by convergence in probability.

Candidate limit: \(X=X_1\).

Try Press Play. The wave gets faster and faster, yet one of the four views does not change at all. Which, and why?

Almost surely: no. For almost every \(\omega\), \(\cos(2\pi n\omega)\) keeps wandering over \([-1,1]\).

In probability: no. \(\mathbb E[(X_n-X_m)^2]=1\) for all \(n\ne m\), so the sequence is not closing in on anything.

In distribution: yes. Cut \([0,1]\) into \(n\) equal pieces; on each, \(X_n\) is a copy of \(X_1\). So every \(X_n\) has the same distribution function.
