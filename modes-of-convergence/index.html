Modes of convergence

ST229 · Chapter 8 · Lectures 17 and 18 · one sequence of random variables, seen four ways
  • Def 8.2Almost surely
    \(\mathbb P\big(\lim_{n\to\infty}X_n=X\big)=1\)
    Limit inside \(\mathbb P\). One outcome at a time: almost every row of the map is eventually clear.
  • \(\Rightarrow\)
  • Def 8.1In probability
    \(\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb P\big(|X_n-X|>\varepsilon\big)=0\ \ \forall\varepsilon>0\)
    Limit outside \(\mathbb P\). One time at a time: the amber share of a column tends to 0.
  • \(\Rightarrow\)
  • Def 8.3In distribution
    \(F_{X_n}(x)\to F_X(x)\ \text{ at continuity points of } F_X\)

A real sequence converges if, for any tolerance \(\varepsilon>0\), it is eventually within \(\varepsilon\) of its limit. Each mode asks that of a different real sequence built from \((X_n)\).

Seven examples, each with one job

The point. Convergence in probability looks at one time \(n\) at a time. The probability of being more than \(\varepsilon\) from the limit, \(\mathbb P(|\bar X_n-\mu|>\varepsilon)\), is an ordinary real sequence, and it tends to 0. This is the weak law of large numbers.

\[\bar X_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i,\qquad X=\mu,\]

where \(X_1,X_2,\dots\) are independent fair coin flips (0 or 1), so \(\mu=\tfrac12\) and \(\sigma^2=\tfrac14\). An outcome \(\omega\) is a whole sequence of flips; the first view shows 300 of them.

Try Press Play and watch the amber thin out, column by column. The solid amber curve is its exact share; the dashed curve is the Chebyshev bound \(\sigma^2/(n\varepsilon^2)\) from the proof. Now make \(\varepsilon\) smaller: the amber lasts longer, but the share still tends to 0. It must do so for every \(\varepsilon\).

In probability: yes (Theorem 8.1, WLLN). \(\mathbb E[\bar X_n]=\mu\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)=\sigma^2/n\), so by Chebyshev \(\mathbb P(|\bar X_n-\mu|\ge\varepsilon)\le\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2}\to0\).

Almost surely: yes (Theorem 8.2, SLLN). With probability 1 the violet path is a convergent real sequence. This is a stronger statement and needs a different proof.

In distribution: yes. \(F_{\bar X_n}\) tends to the step at \(\mu\), the distribution function of the constant \(\mu\).

The point. Almost sure convergence looks at one outcome at a time. Fix \(\omega\) and ask whether \(X_1(\omega),X_2(\omega),\dots\) converges as an ordinary real sequence. Here it does for every \(\omega\) except \(\omega=1\), and that exception has probability 0. That is what “almost” allows.

\[X_n(\omega)=\omega^n,\qquad X=0,\]

with \(\omega\) uniform on \([0,1]\).

Try In the map, every row is amber for a while and then clear for good, except the very top edge \(\omega=1\). Drag \(\omega\) towards 1: the violet sequence takes longer and longer to enter the band, but it always does. Make \(\varepsilon\) smaller and check again.

Almost surely: yes. For every \(\omega<1\), \(\omega^n\to0\). The only exception is \(\omega=1\), a set of probability 0, which is why we do not ask for every \(\omega\).

In probability: yes. \(\mathbb P(\omega^n>\varepsilon)=1-\varepsilon^{1/n}\to0\).

In distribution: yes. \(F_{X_n}(x)=x^{1/n}\to1\) for every \(x\in(0,1]\).

The point. Convergence in distribution looks only at distribution functions. \(F_{nM_n}(x)\to1-e^{-x}\) at every \(x\), so \(nM_n\) converges in distribution to \(\text{Exp}(1)\). Nothing is compared outcome by outcome; there is not even an \(X(\omega)\) to compare with.

\[nM_n=\min_{i\le n}nX_i,\qquad \text{limit: } \text{Exp}(1).\]

\(\mathbb P(nM_n>x)=\big(1-\tfrac xn\big)^n\to e^{-x}\).

Try Raise \(n\) and watch the green curve settle on the dashed \(1-e^{-x}\). Drag \(x\) to check any point you like. Then look at the violet view: does a single outcome settle? Does the definition care?

In distribution: yes. \(F_{nM_n}(x)=1-\big(1-\tfrac xn\big)^n\to1-e^{-x}\), the distribution function of \(\text{Exp}(1)\).

Almost surely and in probability: not asked. The limit here is only a distribution; there is no \(X(\omega)\) on the same space to compare with. The violet path keeps jumping: the definition of convergence in distribution never looks at single outcomes.

The point. If almost every sequence eventually stays within \(\varepsilon\) of the limit, then at a late time \(n\) only a few outcomes can still be outside. Here that is easy to see, because \(M_n(\omega)\) only ever decreases: an outcome is amber at time \(n\) exactly when its sequence has not yet entered the band. So the amber set can only shrink, and it shrinks to nothing.

\[M_n=\min_{i\le n}X_i,\qquad X_i\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}U[0,1],\qquad X=0.\]

\(\mathbb P(M_n\le x)=1-(1-x)^n\) for \(x\in[0,1]\).

Try In the map every row is amber up to some time and clear for ever after. Step \(n\) forward with +1: amber dots in the column view turn grey and never turn back. Rows clearing one after another is exactly what makes the column share fall.

Almost surely: yes. \(M_n(\omega)\) is decreasing in \(n\), and with probability 1 some \(X_i\) falls below any given \(\varepsilon\). After that the sequence stays in the band.

In probability: yes, as a consequence. The amber set at time \(n\) is the set of outcomes not yet in the band. These sets decrease to a set of probability 0, so \(\mathbb P(M_n>\varepsilon)=(1-\varepsilon)^n\to0\). The general proof replaces “outside at time \(n\)” by “outside at some time \(\ge n\)”, which is also decreasing.

In distribution: yes. \(1-(1-x)^n\to1\) for every \(x>0\), and it is 0 for \(x<0\): the distribution function of \(X\equiv0\).

The point. If \(X_n\) is within \(\varepsilon\) of \(c\) with probability close to 1, then for \(x>c+\varepsilon\) almost all the probability lies below \(x\), so \(F_{X_n}(x)\) is close to 1; for \(x

\[X_n\sim N\!\Big(c+\tfrac1n,\ \tfrac1{n^2}\Big),\qquad X=c,\qquad\text{here } c=1.\]

Built on \([0,1]\) as \(X_n(\omega)=c+\dfrac{1+\Phi^{-1}(\omega)}{n}\).

Try Raise \(n\): the distribution concentrates on \(c\). The dashed amber curve is the bound \(2/(\varepsilon^2n^2)\). Then drag \(x\) in the green view onto \(c\) exactly. Does \(F_{X_n}(c)\) approach \(F_X(c)=1\)?

In probability: yes. \(\mathbb E[(X_n-c)^2]=\tfrac1{n^2}+\tfrac1{n^2}=\tfrac2{n^2}\), so \(\mathbb P(|X_n-c|>\varepsilon)\le\dfrac{2}{\varepsilon^2n^2}\to0\).

In distribution: yes, as a consequence. For \(x>c\): \(\mathbb P(X_n\le x)\ge\mathbb P(|X_n-c|\le|x-c|)\to1\). For \(x|x-c|)\to0\). At \(x=c\): \(F_{X_n}(c)=\Phi(-1)\approx0.16\) for every \(n\), not \(1=F_X(c)\), but \(c\) is not a continuity point of \(F_X\).

Almost surely: yes, for this construction. \(X_n(\omega)\to c\) for every \(\omega\in(0,1)\).

The point. Having the same distribution says nothing about being close outcome by outcome. Every \(X_n\) is \(\text{Unif}[0,1]\), so the distribution functions do not move at all, yet \(X_n(\omega)\) and \(X_{n+1}(\omega)\) are far apart for most \(\omega\). So in distribution \(\not\Rightarrow\) in probability.

\[X_{2k}=U,\qquad X_{2k+1}=1-U,\]

with \(U\sim\text{Unif}[0,1]\) picked once and for all; here \(U(\omega)=\omega\). The views compare \(X_n\) with the candidate limit \(X=U\).

Try Step \(n\) with +1 and −1. The green view never moves. The map is striped: every other column is mostly amber, for ever, so no column share tends to 0 and no row clears.

In distribution: yes. \(X_n\sim\text{Unif}[0,1]\) for every \(n\), so \(F_{X_n}\) never changes.

In probability: no. \(|X_n-X_{n+1}|=|2U-1|>\varepsilon\) with probability \(1-\varepsilon\), for every \(n\). So \(X_n\) and \(X_{n+1}\) cannot both be close to the same \(X\), whatever \(X\) is.

Almost surely: no. For every \(\omega\ne\tfrac12\) the sequence alternates between \(\omega\) and \(1-\omega\). \(X_n(\omega)\) conspires to differ from \(X_{n+1}(\omega)\) even though they have the same distribution.

The point. Rare is not the same as never again. At each time \(n\) the block where \(X_n=1\) is small, so \(\mathbb P(|X_n|>\varepsilon)\to0\). But the block keeps sweeping across \([0,1]\), so every single outcome is hit again and again, for ever, and no sequence \(X_n(\omega)\) converges. So in probability \(\not\Rightarrow\) almost surely, which is why the weak law is weaker than the strong law.

\[X_n=\mathbf 1_{A_n},\qquad X=0,\]

where \(A_n\) is an interval of length \(\tfrac1{n+1}\) that starts where \(A_{n-1}\) ended and wraps around \([0,1]\).

Try In the map the block is a thin amber stripe climbing through the outcomes again and again. Read it by columns: less and less amber. Read it by rows: follow any row to the right and it keeps being hit. Press Play and watch your chosen \(\omega\) each time the block comes round.

Almost surely: no. The lengths \(\tfrac1{n+1}\) sum to infinity, so the block laps \([0,1]\) for ever and covers every \(\omega\) infinitely often.

In probability: yes. \(\mathbb P(|X_n|>\varepsilon)=\tfrac1{n+1}\to0\) for \(\varepsilon<1\). Large errors become rare, but never stop.

In distribution: yes. Implied by convergence in probability.

The point. Not every sequence of distributions converges. Here the probability mass escapes to infinity and the pointwise limit of the distribution functions is not a distribution function.

\[X_n\sim\text{Unif}\{1,2,\dots,n\}\]

built here as \(X_n(\omega)=\lceil n\omega\rceil\); the dependence between different \(n\) does not matter for the distribution.

Try Raise \(n\) and drag \(x\) in the green view. What does \(F_{X_n}(x)\) tend to, for each fixed \(x\)? Is the limit a distribution function?

In distribution: no. \(\mathbb P(X_n=x)=\tfrac1n\to0\) for each \(x\), and \(F_{X_n}(x)=\tfrac{\lfloor x\rfloor}{n}\to0\) for every \(x\). The limit is not a distribution function: probability mass is escaping to infinity.

Almost surely and in probability: no. \(X_n(\omega)\to\infty\) for every \(\omega>0\).

The point. Discrete random variables can converge in distribution to a continuous one. Mass functions are the wrong thing to compare; distribution functions are the right thing.

\[Y_n=\frac{X_n}{n}\in[0,1],\qquad X_n\sim\text{Unif}\{1,\dots,n\};\qquad \text{limit: } \text{Unif}[0,1].\]

Built here as \(Y_n(\omega)=\lceil n\omega\rceil/n\). The grey spikes in the green view are the mass function, height \(\tfrac1n\).

Try Raise \(n\) slowly. The spikes shrink to 0, yet the probability of an interval does not vanish, and the green staircase closes in on the diagonal.

In distribution: yes. \(F_{Y_n}(y)=\tfrac{\lfloor ny\rfloor}{n}\), and \(y-\tfrac1n\le\tfrac{\lfloor ny\rfloor}{n}\le y\), so \(F_{Y_n}(y)\to y\) on \([0,1]\). Discrete random variables with a continuous limit.

Almost surely and in probability: yes, for this construction. \(|Y_n(\omega)-\omega|<\tfrac1n\) for every \(\omega\). The example in the notes only asks about the distribution.

The point. A second example of in probability but not almost surely, this time with independent noise and the second Borel–Cantelli lemma.

\[X_n=\frac{\xi_n}{n},\qquad X=0,\]

where \(\xi_1,\xi_2,\dots\) are independent standard Cauchy.

Try Follow one outcome in the violet view all the way to \(n=400\). Large errors get rarer. Do they stop? Compare with the next example.

Almost surely: no. \(\sum_n\mathbb P(|X_n|>\varepsilon)=\infty\) and the events are independent, so by the second Borel–Cantelli lemma \(|X_n|>\varepsilon\) happens infinitely often with probability 1.

In probability: yes. \(\mathbb P(|X_n|>\varepsilon)=1-\tfrac2\pi\arctan(n\varepsilon)\to0\).

In distribution: yes. Implied by convergence in probability.

The point. Damp the same noise faster and almost sure convergence returns, by the first Borel–Cantelli lemma.

\[X_n=\frac{\xi_n}{n^2},\qquad X=0,\]

where \(\xi_1,\xi_2,\dots\) are independent standard Cauchy.

Try Compare the amber curve with the previous example: both fall to 0. What is different about how fast, and what does that do to a single outcome?

Almost surely: yes. \(\sum_n\mathbb P(|X_n|>\varepsilon)<\infty\), so by the first Borel–Cantelli lemma \(|X_n|>\varepsilon\) happens only finitely often, with probability 1.

In probability: yes. Implied by almost sure convergence.

In distribution: yes. Implied by convergence in probability.

The point. A second example of in distribution only: identical distributions for every \(n\), while the functions themselves never settle.

\[X_n(\omega)=\cos(2\pi n\omega)\]

Candidate limit: \(X=X_1\).

Try Press Play. The wave gets faster and faster, yet one of the four views does not change at all. Which, and why?

Almost surely: no. For almost every \(\omega\), \(\cos(2\pi n\omega)\) keeps wandering over \([-1,1]\).

In probability: no. \(\mathbb E[(X_n-X_m)^2]=1\) for all \(n\ne m\), so the sequence is not closing in on anything.

In distribution: yes. Cut \([0,1]\) into \(n\) equal pieces; on each, \(X_n\) is a copy of \(X_1\). So every \(X_n\) has the same distribution function.
